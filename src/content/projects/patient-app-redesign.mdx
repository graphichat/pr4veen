# Patient App Redesign

HealthPlix is a clinical technology company whose core product helps doctors manage their practice — prescription writing, patient records, consultation history. The patient-facing app is the other half of that equation: it gives patients access to their prescriptions, lets them book appointments, and keeps them connected to their doctor between visits.

When I worked on this project, the patient app had been live for a while. It worked. But the numbers told a different story — only 15% of registered patients were using it in any given month, and the support team was fielding a steady stream of tickets about things that should have been obvious. Patients were struggling to understand their own health data, features that could have made a real difference were going undiscovered, and the app was doing very little to bring people back after their first visit.

The brief I took on was to redesign it from the ground up.

## Understanding Why Patients Weren't Engaging

Before touching the interface, I needed to understand the gap between what the app was offering and what patients actually needed from it. I interviewed 40 patients across a range of demographics, health conditions, and comfort with technology. Some were managing chronic conditions and checking in frequently. Others had downloaded the app after a one-time visit and never opened it again.

![Patient App — early research and concept exploration in Figma, mapping flows and information structure before high-fidelity work began](/images/projects/iqhealth/patient-app-research.png)

The picture that came out of the research was consistent. Patients found the app passive — it stored records, but it didn't help them understand them. Medical terminology used in prescription summaries and test results was often opaque. The navigation assumed familiarity with a clinical data model that most patients didn't have. People weren't discovering appointment booking or medication reminders because they weren't looking for them in the places the app had put them.

There was also a trust dimension. Patients who were unfamiliar with digital health tools were cautious. An interface that felt clinical and complicated reinforced that caution. The design needed to earn trust by making things feel simple and clear, not by adding explanations on top of existing complexity.

I ran a broader survey with 500+ patients to pressure-test the qualitative findings and segment by usage pattern. The analytics also pointed to a specific set of drop-off moments — points in the app where users arrived and then left without doing what the flow was designed for.

## The Design Direction

The research crystallised a few core principles that shaped the redesign. The first was visual over textual: where the app had displayed raw clinical data, the redesign would use charts, timelines, and structured summaries that communicated meaning rather than just transferring information. The second was proactive over passive — the app needed to reach out, not just sit there waiting to be opened. The third was mobile-first, not mobile-adapted. The original app had been designed for a desktop mental model and then squeezed into a phone. The redesign treated the phone as the primary context from the start.

These weren't just design principles — they were direct responses to what patients had told us.

## What Changed

The home screen was the biggest shift. The original version was a list of links to sections of the app. The redesign replaced it with a contextual health dashboard — a surface that surfaced upcoming appointments, recent test results, and active medication schedules in one view, with visual treatments that made the information readable at a glance. Patients no longer needed to navigate to find out what was relevant to them right now; the app surfaced it.

Navigation was restructured around the tasks patients actually wanted to do — book an appointment, see a prescription, check a result — rather than around the way clinical data was organised on the backend. Bottom navigation handled the primary actions. Secondary features were available contextually, but they weren't competing for attention on the main interface.

The appointment booking flow was redesigned as a visual calendar with clear doctor availability, one-tap rescheduling, and confirmation states that gave patients confidence the booking had gone through. The previous flow had a few too many ambiguous states that were generating a disproportionate share of support queries.

Health records got a significant rework. Test results and prescription summaries now displayed with plain language summaries alongside the clinical data — not replacing the source record, but helping patients orient before they read the detail. A timeline view of health history gave patients a sense of continuity rather than a stack of disconnected records.

![Patient App — prototype flows covering Login, Home, Appointment Booking, Patient Profile, Online Consultation, and Clinic Check-In](/images/projects/iqhealth/patient-app-prototype.png)

The prototype stage covered the full set of primary flows: login and onboarding, the home dashboard, appointment booking, patient profile, online consultation, and clinic check-in. Each flow was tested with patients before moving to final design — the booking and consultation flows went through two rounds of iteration based on usability testing feedback.

## Results

After the redesign shipped, monthly active users increased by 35%. Support queries dropped by 45%. Appointment bookings through the app went up 28%. In usability testing, information comprehension scores improved significantly — patients were reading their health data and understanding it, rather than opening the app, getting confused, and closing it again.

The qualitative feedback reflected that too. Patients who had found the original app confusing described the redesigned version as something they actually wanted to open. That shift — from a tool you use because you have to, to something you return to — was what the project was really trying to achieve.

## Reflections

What this project reinforced for me is that healthcare UX fails most often not because of bad design execution, but because the design started from the wrong frame of reference. Clinical tools are built from a data model — the records, the test types, the prescription structure. When that same model becomes the navigation and the layout of a patient-facing app, it asks patients to understand their health through a system that was built for clinicians. Patients don't need less information. They need the same information reframed around their context: *what does this mean for me, what do I need to do next, and when do I need to come back.*

That reframing — from data display to patient communication — was the core design problem, and everything else followed from it.
